the liver & the stars

In Babylon a diviner read the gods' answer in the liver of a sheep, then looked to the night sky. A liver has been drawn for you. No two are alike.

I. the liver
The gods have left marks on it. Move slowly and click where you see one.
I. the liver, read

What the liver says

    Marks on the right speak about you. Marks on the left speak about whoever, or whatever, stands against you.

    The diviner

    The specialist was called a bārû, "one who inspects". Before the sheep was killed he prayed to Shamash, the sun god and judge, and to Adad, the storm god, asking them to place a true answer inside the animal.

    The liver as a tablet

    Diviners spoke of the liver as a tablet the gods had written on. Its surface was divided into named places: the Presence, the Path, the Palace Gate, the Finger, the Yoke and others. This site's liver is drawn after a clay model in the British Museum, made in the Old Babylonian period, roughly 1900 to 1600 BC, and probably from Sippar. Each box on that model gives the meaning of a blemish found at that spot.

    Right and left

    The right side of a feature concerned the person asking. The left concerned the enemy. A bad mark on the left was therefore good news, which is why a hole on the left counts in your favour here.

    Kings, armies, and you

    The omen collections mostly speak of kings, armies and city gates, because the best-recorded clients were kings. Under each sign, the first line is in that old voice and the second puts it into everyday terms.

    Draft status: the old-voice omen lines are paraphrases written in the style of the omen collections, not quotations, and the two marks used (Hole and Weapon) follow a simplified rule. They are placeholders until checked translations replace them.

    II. the sky
    Some of these stars belong to constellations. Search the sky, and choose three.
    II. the sky, read

    What the sky says

      A constellation that shines brightly speaks for you. One that is faint speaks against you.

      Signs sent unasked

      Signs in the sky were warnings the gods sent without being asked, collected in the series Enūma Anu Enlil. The constellation names used here come from the Babylonian star list MUL.APIN.

      Sky and liver together

      Assyrian kings had worrying sky signs checked by liver, so the two crafts did work together. Personal horoscopes arrived much later, around 410 BC, about a thousand years after the clay liver.

      Draft status: the constellation drawings are sketches, not star charts, and the bright-or-faint rule is a simplification. Both are placeholders.

      The signs, weighed

      for you
      against you

        No single mark settled the matter. The diviner tallied favourable signs against unfavourable ones and gave the balance as the answer. When the result was unclear, a second sheep was read as a check.
